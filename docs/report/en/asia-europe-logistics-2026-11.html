1. Key points

  • Suez is back for 58% of Far East–North Europe container capacity; the rest still sails round the Cape. Shanghai–Rotterdam takes 29.6 sea days via Suez, 40.1 via the Cape, 34.0 on today's mix (Alphaliner, mid-October 2026; transit-times.json). Hormuz is de facto closed: 4.4 transits a day against 90.3 a year earlier (IMF PortWatch, week to 1 November 2026).
  • Fuel, carbon and war risk per box are up 74% since late 2023. On Shanghai–Rotterdam they come to about 842 $ per 40-foot container (FEU) today against 483 $ in 2023. The fuel price adds 129 $, EU carbon rules 124 $, the Cape detour 89 $ (section 3; prices of 6 November 2026 and 9 November 2026).
  • Freight rates have risen far more than these costs. The Drewry WCI Shanghai–Rotterdam is 3,615 $/FEU (5 November 2026) against 1,048 $ on 2 November 2023: +2,567 $. Fuel, carbon and war risk explain about 359 $ of that (14%).
  • No North Sea or Baltic port is at high risk. Rotterdam and Antwerp-Bruges score 7.5 of 25 (medium) on the monitor's Port Risk Index: top impact (5.0 of 5), low probability (1.5) (port-risk.json, 10 November 2026). The highest scores are the Russian Gulf of Finland ports, 9.3.
  • Watch the Red Sea war-risk premium (assessment). On fuel and carbon alone, the Cape beats Suez when the premium passes about 0.43–1.09% of hull value, depending on the canal toll; charter costs would raise that line. The last public quote is 0.2–0.3% (24 September 2026, assicurazioni.json).
AssessmentFor Dutch, Danish and Baltic logistics the corridor is now a cost problem more than a capacity problem. Ships are getting through, but each box carries a higher fuel bill and a new carbon bill. The risk dashboard still rates the Red Sea container route high (18.0 of 25, rischio.json). The routing mix (58/42 on North Europe services) still adds about 4.4 days on average, and it could flip back within weeks if the Red Sea relapses. The full return to Suez expected by Q1 2027 would lower costs, but it would also bring back the bunching of arrivals at North Sea hubs seen when routings change.

2. The route map today: Suez back for most Asia–Europe capacity, Cape for the rest, Hormuz closed

Passages, 9 November 2026. Suez is «open, traffic recovering»; Bab el-Mandeb «open, high threat»; Hormuz «de facto closed» (stretti-dettaglio.json, via transit-times.json). IMF PortWatch counts transits of ships with AIS on (the Automatic Identification System, the ship's position transponder). In the week to 1 November 2026: Suez 43.0 a day (+10.3% on a year earlier, −43.4% on three years earlier); Bab el-Mandeb 27.3 (−65.7% on three years); Cape of Good Hope 92.0 (+89.3% on three years); Hormuz 4.4 (−95.1% on three years).

How much is back on Suez. Alphaliner counts 5.50 of 8.70 million TEU deployed on Far East–Europe services using the Red Sea in the month 15 September–15 October 2026: 63% overall, but 58% on Far East–North Europe and 70% on Far East–Mediterranean. Some services use the Red Sea westbound only and the Cape on the way back (Alphaliner, as reported by Ship & Bunker (21 October 2026) and The Loadstar, reliability medium: search-result excerpts read on 10 November 2026; the article could not be opened from the review session). Other measures give lower shares (Sea-Intelligence 27% of capacity on a normalised basis, Maritime Strategies International about 35% of sailings; search excerpts, not checked). Linerlytica expects a full return by Q1 2027 (Seatrade Maritime, in stretti-dettaglio.json, reliability medium). BIMCO counts 753,000 TEU a week through Suez in the first two weeks of October 2026, against 1,457,000 in the same weeks of 2023 (BIMCO, 14 October 2026). This report weights North European and Baltic lanes with 58% via Suez and Mediterranean lanes with 70%; transit-times.json uses 63% for every lane, so its weighted days are slightly lower for the North (Shanghai–Rotterdam 33.5 days there, 34.0 here).

North versus south. The Cape penalty is 10.5 days to Rotterdam but 15.7 to Genoa, 18.7 to Trieste and 19.6 to Piraeus. Gdańsk and Klaipėda sit 881 and 938 nm beyond Rotterdam on the same voyage (2.5 and 2.7 more days), and pass the Danish straits.

Hormuz. Qatari LNG to Rotterdam is «blocked (Hormuz de facto closed)»: Qatar has no export terminal outside the Gulf. Saudi crude piped to Yanbu reaches Rotterdam in 13.2 days, against 21.8 from Ras Tanura via Hormuz, but Yanbu carries a war-risk premium of 3.0% of hull value for Saudi-linked ships (Reuters (four market sources), via Insurance Journal, 24 September 2026). Jebel Ali boxes are trucked to Khor Fakkan on the Gulf of Oman; the sea leg to Rotterdam is 17.2 days (transit-times.json).

AssessmentThe return to Suez helps the Mediterranean about 1.5 to 1.9 times as much as the North Sea in sea days, because the Cape penalty to Genoa or Piraeus is 16–20 days against about 10 to Rotterdam. For Rotterdam, Antwerp, Hamburg and the Baltic feeders the routing question is mainly one of schedule reliability: services are being moved between two routes that differ by 10 days.

3. Cost stack of an Asia–North Europe voyage, today against 2023

One voyage Shanghai → Rotterdam with a container ship of about 15,000 TEU (twenty-foot equivalent units), at 15 knots, burning 150 t of very-low-sulphur fuel oil (VLSFO) a day and carrying 6,375 FEU (15,000 TEU × 85% utilisation ÷ 2). Hull value 150 M$, an in-service ship (a 15,500 TEU newbuild is priced about 222 M$). These are the transit model's declared hypotheses (transit-times.json), not the data of a real ship. Four scenarios: 2023 (Suez, 2023 fuel price, no carbon rules, pre-crisis war risk), today via Suez, today via the Cape, and today weighted 58/42, the Far East–North Europe split.

The calculation

  • Fuel = sea days × 150 t/day × VLSFO price. Suez 29.6 d → 4,440 t; Cape 40.1 d → 6,015 t. Price today: Singapore 854.0 $/t (6 November 2026, Ship & Bunker in bunker-emissioni.json). Price 2023: 668.5 $/t, Singapore VLSFO, October 2023 monthly average (Ship & Bunker monthly price review, as quoted by PortNews (16 November 2023), reliability medium: value confirmed in a search-result excerpt on 10 November 2026; the page itself could not be opened from the review session). Fuel today via Suez: 4,440 × 854.0 = 3.79 M$.
  • EU ETS (the EU Emissions Trading System, which since 2024 makes ships buy allowances for their emissions). It covers 50% of the last voyage into the EU from a non-EU port; a call at Tanger Med or East Port Said does not break the voyage. Assumption: the last non-EU call is Singapore, so the covered leg is Singapore → Rotterdam, 23.6 d via Suez (3,540 t) or 34.0 d via the Cape (5,100 t). Cost per tonne burnt: 3.1631 t CO2e × 50% × 100% phase-in (2026) × 86.91 €/allowance (EEX auction, 9 November 2026) = 137.45 €/t, or 154.25 $/t at 1.1222 $/€. Via Suez: 0.55 M$. In 2023 maritime ETS did not exist (in force from 1 January 2024).
  • FuelEU Maritime (the EU rule capping the greenhouse-gas intensity of ship fuel, in force since 2025). For VLSFO the 2026 limit is exceeded, and the maximum cost is the penalty: 31.10 €/t on the same 50% leg (bunker-emissioni.json, example «asia-rotterdam»). Via Suez: 0.12 M$. Pooling or bio-blends can cost less; zero in 2023.
  • War risk = premium × hull value. Today via Suez: 0.25% × 150 M$ = 0.38 M$ (midpoint of the 0.2–0.3% quoted for tankers by Reuters (four market sources), via Insurance Journal, 24 September 2026). Via the Cape: no listed area on the route, so zero. 2023: 0.075%, the pre-crisis level (less than 0.1% in late 2023, Moody's via assicurazioni.json) = 0.11 M$.
  • Per FEU = voyage cost ÷ 6,375 FEU. Weighted = 58% × Suez + 42% × Cape.

Reading. The stack rises from 483 to 842 $/FEU (+74%). Fuel price adds 129 $, the Cape share 89 $, carbon rules 124 $ together, war risk 16 $. Since the eve of the Iran war, the Suez voyage's fuel bill alone has grown by 1.48 M$ (Singapore VLSFO 521.5 $/t on 27 February 2026), the same figure as in transit-times.json.

When does the Cape pay? Via the Cape the voyage costs 1.64 M$ more in fuel, ETS and FuelEU than via Suez. The Cape avoids the Red Sea premium and the canal toll. Without the toll, the break-even premium is 1.09% of a 150 M$ hull; with a toll of 0.5 M$–1.0 M$ it falls to 0.43–0.76%. These break-even values are lower bounds: the charter hire or capital cost of 10.5 more days at sea is not counted, and it would raise the line.

Why assicurazioni.json finds a lower line. Its example gives 0.32%: UNCTAD's 2024 extra Cape cost of 0.7 M$ for a mid-size container ship at 2024 fuel prices, divided by the 221.7 M$ price of a new 15,500 TEU ship. Both inputs differ from this model. The cost base is 2.3 times larger here (a 15,000 TEU ship at 2026 fuel prices, with ETS and FuelEU); the hull is 1.5 times smaller. With this model's cost on a 221.7 M$ hull the line is 0.74% without the toll and 0.29–0.51% with it. The hull value matters for the premium bill too: the Northern Europe report prices one Red Sea premium on the 221.7 M$ newbuild, 0.55 M$ (0.49 M€) at 0.25%, against 0.38 M$ here.

AssessmentCarriers decide the route on the whole voyage economics (charter cost of ten more days, schedule, crew safety), not on this stack alone. Because charter is left out, a Red Sea premium of about 0.5% is where the Cape starts to compete for an in-service ship, not where it clearly wins. The quotes of 21–23 July 2026 (0.75% to over 1%) were above that line.

What drives the rate. (1) Capacity released by the Suez return. Each ship moved back from the Cape to Suez saves about 10.5 days each way to North Europe, so the same fleet offers more weekly slots. (2) Fuel. Singapore VLSFO is up 63.8% since 27 February 2026 and 82.7% on a year earlier (bunker-emissioni.json). (3) Carbon. Maersk charges 170 $/FEU from the Far East to North Europe for ETS and FuelEU; the model's own ETS + FuelEU cost is 124 $/FEU at today's EUA. A surcharge is a price, not a cost: it can include other lanes, feeder legs and a margin. (4) War risk, now 34 $/FEU in the weighted case. (5) The Mediterranean premium: Shanghai–Genoa costs 15.2% more than Shanghai–Rotterdam, inside the 5–20% band the risk dashboard reads as «gradual return under threat» (rischio.json, 9 November 2026).

AssessmentSpot rates are drifting down (FBX global from its August high, FBX11 down on the week) while the CCFI, which also reflects contract rates, edges up. The cost floor computed here, about 840 $/FEU for fuel, carbon and war risk, is 23% of today's WCI. That leaves room for spot rates to fall as Cape capacity returns. Fuel would have to rise sharply to stop that.

6. Alternatives: rail, air and nearshoring

China–Europe rail. 20,022 trains in 2025 (+3.2% on 2024), carrying more than 2.05 million TEU (−1.3% on 2024); 5,460 trains in Q1 2026 (+29% on Q1 2025) (CGTN, Railmarket, Index1520, in ferrovie.json; TEU figure reliability medium). The northern route crosses Russia and Belarus, with sanctions and border risk at the Belarus–Poland crossing.

Middle Corridor (Trans-Caspian route via Kazakhstan, the Caspian, Azerbaijan and Georgia): 4.7 million tonnes in 2025 (Organisation of Turkic States via BESA; other 2025 estimates 4.5–5.2 Mt), target 11.4 Mt by 2030 (reliability low). INSTC (North–South corridor, Russia–Iran–India): 12.9 Mt by rail in 2024; the start of construction on the missing Rasht–Astara link has slipped to 2027 (Interfax, 15 October 2026). It runs through Iran, so it is no alternative for EU shippers under current sanctions.

AssessmentRail is a niche for time-sensitive cargo, not a substitute. Some 2.05 million TEU a year is about 39,000 TEU a week, roughly 5% of the capacity that passed Suez each week in early October 2026. The two figures measure different things: loaded boxes on trains against ship capacity. Missing: rail transit times and $/FEU rates for 2026.

Air for high-value cargo: chips and lithography. Every EUV (extreme-ultraviolet) lithography scanner in the world is assembled by ASML at Veldhoven, near Eindhoven; ASML sold 113 new lithography systems in Q3 2026 with revenue of 11.7 billion € (ASML results, 14 October 2026). One EUV machine travels as about 40 containers, 20 trucks and three Boeing 747 freighters (Asia Times, 2021, reliability medium); a High-NA EXE:5000 went to Oregon in 43 containers. Schiphol as the departure airport is an assessment: ASML does not publish the airports it uses (supply-chain-ai.json). Chips and AI servers fly because they are light and valuable: AI-related cargo is 40–50% of EVA Air's freight (DIGITIMES, reliability medium). Missing: an air-cargo rate series (Asia–Europe $/kg) in the repository.

Dependence that a shorter route does not remove. EU imports from outside the EU of NdFeB permanent magnets: 88.3% from China; artificial graphite 66.1% from China (UN Comtrade 2025, commercio.json). China's share of total imports in 2025: Germany 12.9%, Poland 15.5%, the Netherlands 8.3% (same file; Dutch figures include goods re-exported through Rotterdam).

AssessmentNearshoring signals. The repository holds no data that measures nearshoring directly: no import share series by origin over time, no factory-announcement tracker. What it shows is consistent with hedging, not relocation: rail volumes up while sea capacity returns, carriers adding Mediterranean transhipment (Tanger Med 11.1 million TEU in 2025, +8.4%, Port Strategy in stretti-dettaglio.json), and dependencies on China for magnets and graphite still above 60%. For a logistics employer, the practical consequence is more routing options to price, not fewer Asian boxes.

7. Sanctions and the shadow fleet: compliance risk for shippers and ports

The lists. 2,022 ships are sanctioned by at least one of the US (OFAC, 1,570), the UK (683) and the EU (734); 275 by all three. 950 are linked to Russia and 679 to Iran; 1,163 are tankers (or of unknown type) sanctioned for Russia or Iran, which the monitor counts as probable shadow fleet (sanzioni-navi.json: OFAC 6 November 2026, UK 3 November 2026, EU via OpenSanctions 28 October 2026). 672 Russia-linked ships are on EU lists, which include the port-access ban of Annex XLII to Regulation 833/2014; the file does not say how many fall under that ban.

Container ships are on the lists too. 59 container ships are sanctioned: 55 linked to Iran and 4 to Russia, all by the US only. A box on one of them can breach US secondary-sanctions rules even when the EU lists do not name the ship. New first designations in the last 90 days: 6 ships with a known date, between 1 October 2026 and 2 October 2026 (UK, USA; scope Russia; types: LNG Carrier, LNG Tanker, type not given) (sanzioni-navi.json). The OFAC list gives no designation dates, so ships listed by the US alone are not counted in that figure.

Baltic exposure. In the AIS snapshot of 9 November 2026 38 of 96 tankers seen in the Gulf of Finland were sanctioned; in the Danish straits 0 of 19 (navi.json). The Port Risk Index counts likely shadow-fleet or sanctioned tankers within 50 km of Ust-Luga (22), Primorsk (17), St Petersburg (6), Tallinn (2) (port-risk.json). Russia remains a Joint War Committee listed area (war-risk cover needs notice and an additional premium) in the circular in force, JWLA-035 of 16 September 2026.

AssessmentFor shippers and ports in the Netherlands, Denmark and the Baltic the compliance risk sits in three places. (1) Screening by IMO number, not by name: sanctioned ships change name and flag often, and the flag is frequently false. (2) US-only listings: the container ships on the lists are almost all US designations for Iran, which an EU-only screening misses. (3) Ship-to-ship transfers and bunkering near the Gulf of Finland and Skagen, where sanctioned tankers meet others. Ports are also exposed operationally: an old, poorly insured tanker at anchor near a cable or a fairway is a spill and liability risk.

9. Glossary

  • TEU / FEU: twenty-foot / forty-foot equivalent unit, the standard container sizes; one FEU = 2 TEU. Freight rates are quoted per FEU.
  • VLSFO: very-low-sulphur fuel oil (0.5% sulphur), the standard marine fuel since 2020, priced in $ per tonne at bunkering hubs such as Singapore and Rotterdam.
  • EU ETS / EUA: the EU Emissions Trading System; ships calling at EU ports must surrender one EU Allowance (EUA) per tonne of CO2-equivalent, for 50% of voyages into or out of the EU and 100% within it.
  • FuelEU Maritime: EU rule capping the greenhouse-gas intensity of ship fuel from 2025; ships above the limit pay a penalty or pool with cleaner ships.
  • War risk / additional premium: war cover for the hull, bought separately; entering a Joint War Committee (JWC) listed area requires notice and an additional premium, quoted in % of hull value per transit (usually 7 days).
  • Hull value: the insured value of the ship; the premium bill is premium × hull value.
  • Break-even premium: the war-risk premium at which the Cape detour and the Suez route cost the same in this model.
  • WCI / FBX / CCFI: Drewry World Container Index (weekly spot rates), Freightos Baltic Index (spot), China Containerized Freight Index (Shanghai Shipping Exchange, spot and contract rates).
  • AIS / PortWatch: the Automatic Identification System transponder; IMF PortWatch turns AIS positions into daily port calls and chokepoint transits.
  • Blank sailing: a scheduled departure a carrier cancels, usually to match capacity to demand or after a routing change.
  • Shadow fleet: old tankers with opaque ownership and insurance carrying sanctioned Russian or Iranian oil; here, tankers (or ships of unknown type) sanctioned for Russia or Iran.
  • OFAC: the US Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control, which keeps the SDN sanctions list.
