1. Summary

  • Suez is recovering, Bab el-Mandeb stays at just over a third, Hormuz is closed in practice. Ships per day with AIS on: Suez 43.0, 58% of the January–October 2023 average; Bab el-Mandeb 27.3 (37%); Hormuz 4.4 (IMF PortWatch, 7-day average 26 October–1 November 2026). On the risk dashboard Hormuz is at critical level, Bab el-Mandeb high, Suez medium.
  • Container rates more than doubled, Italian ports moving at two speeds. Drewry WCI at 4,389 dollars per FEU (5 November 2026), +124% year on year; FBX global +3% over 4 weeks. Over 30 days Ravenna (−20%) and Venice Malamocco (−16%), energy and bulk ports, are down; Genoa −5% (IMF PortWatch, to 30 October 2026).
  • Energy remains the heaviest cost. TTF gas at €82.33/MWh, +164% year on year (Yahoo Finance, 9 November 2026); self-service diesel at €2.224 a litre (Mimit, fuel price observatory (national self-service average), cited by Affaritaliani, 7 November 2026); Italian gas storage 88.9% full (GIE AGSI+, 8 November 2026).
  • Northern Europe: less exposed on freight, more exposed on gas. China–Northern Europe 3,422 dollars per FEU against 4,324 to the Mediterranean (Freightos, 6 November 2026); storage 63.5% full in the Netherlands and 58.9% in Germany, well below the 5-year average (GIE AGSI+). Details in section 8.
  • Critical materials: Chinese decision on 10 November. The suspension of the rare-earth export controls decided in October 2025 expires; as of 9 November no extension had been announced (CIRS Group (text of announcement no. 70/2025) and Mining Technology, 9 November 2026).
AssessmentEurope’s supply chain in November 2026 runs at two speeds. Containers are returning towards Suez and spot rates are no longer rising, but they remain about double a year ago, while energy, fertilisers and critical materials stay tied to Hormuz and China. For Italy the most concrete risk over the next two months is not logistics but cost: gas, diesel and electricity in winter with low European storage.

Facts. Before the Red Sea crisis (January–October 2023 average) Suez handled 74 ships a day and the Cape 49. Since December 2023 the ratio has reversed: Suez’s lowest monthly figure since 2024 is 34.1 (February 2025), the Cape’s highest 98.1 (August 2025). In the latest week measured the Cape is at 92.0 ships a day, 188% of the January–October 2023 average (IMF PortWatch, 7-day average 26 October–1 November 2026). Suez is at 58% and Bab el-Mandeb at 37% of that average. At Hormuz PortWatch sees 4.4 ships a day; Lloyd’s List Intelligence, which also reconstructs transits with the transponder off, counts 116 transits by non-Iran-linked ships in the week 19–25 October 2026, about 17 a day (Lloyd’s List Intelligence, Strait of Hormuz Brief, 5 November 2026). On the risk dashboard Hormuz is at critical level (22.5 out of 25), Bab el-Mandeb high (19.0 out of 25) and Suez medium (9.9 out of 25) (rischio.json, 9 November 2026; details in section 9).

Other sources confirm container ships returning to Suez: container capacity: 753,000 TEU a week in the first two weeks of October 2026, against 1,457,000 in the same period of 2023: above 50% of pre-crisis levels for the first time (BIMCO, Shipping Number of the Week, on Clarksons data); transits: 1,195 transits and 111 million dwt from 28 September to 25 October 2026; tonnage still −36% on the period before the Houthis (Lloyd’s List Intelligence, Red Sea Brief of 29 October 2026).

Time and cost of the Cape diversion. Compared with Suez: about 7,000 km and 14–24 more days; a mid-sized container ship costs about 1 million dollars via Suez and 1.7 million via the Cape (about 160 dollars more per FEU) (UNCTAD, Review of Maritime Transport 2024, chapter 2). For a tanker: diverting an Aframax from Asia to Northern Europe via the Cape costs about 933,000 dollars more (+110%) and lengthens the voyage from 16 to 32 days; +35% for a large container ship (Riviera Maritime Media, on LSEG Shipping Research: “Diversions from Red Sea add US$1M per voyage”).

State of Bab el-Mandeb. UKMTO: no attacks in the Red Sea since 24 August 2026, but more radio contacts from self-styled “Yemeni authorities”; suspicious pirate activity in the Gulf of Aden on 28 October. (Lloyd’s List Intelligence, 29 October 2026). Transits at Bab el-Mandeb: 295 in the week 12–18 October (−8% week on week), tonnage −46% on the period before the Houthis. (Lloyd’s List Intelligence, 18 October 2026).

AssessmentThe return to Suez is real but partial and fragile. Container ships are coming back, while Saudi crude leaving Yanbu makes Bab el-Mandeb a “second Hormuz”: a successful attack on a tanker would send ships back round the Cape within days. The figure to watch is Bab el-Mandeb, not Suez.

AssessmentThe sharpest falls are in Italian ports tied to energy and Adriatic dry bulk (Ravenna, Venice, Trieste). Hormuz alone explains little: only 5.6–10.2% of the crude Italy imports comes from the Persian Gulf (UN Comtrade 2025); weak industrial demand and expensive energy are more likely, but the PortWatch data cannot tell. Genoa and Gioia Tauro see more container ships over 7 days, consistent with the return to Suez, but over 30 days total calls remain below last year. Calls count ships, not tonnes or TEU: volumes require data from the port system authorities, which come out months later.

6. Critical materials and bottlenecks

Rare earths. The suspension of the Chinese rare-earth export controls decided in October 2025 (MOFCOM–GAC announcement no. 70 of 2025, including the rule on 0.1% Chinese content in foreign products) expires on 10 November 2026; the April 2025 licences on seven medium and heavy rare earths (including dysprosium and yttrium) remain in force. As of the date of the report no extension had been announced (CIRS Group (text of announcement no. 70/2025) and Mining Technology, 9 November 2026). For the EU the dependency is almost total: China supplies 100% of the heavy rare earths used in the EU, Türkiye 98% of borate, South Africa 71% of platinum (European Commission, press release on the Critical Raw Materials Act (IP/23/1661), 16 March 2023).

Chips and AI. S&P Global Mobility expects DRAM prices +70–100% in 2026 on new contracts, as producers shift capacity towards chips for AI data centres; Micron (June 2026) says memory supply will stay tight beyond 2027 (S&P Global Mobility (December 2025); Counterpoint Research on Micron (June 2026), June 2026). On the stock market the SOX semiconductor index is at 10,046 points, −20% in a month but +42% year to date; TSMC −14% in a month (Yahoo Finance, 9 November 2026). The Taiwan Strait: companies that depend on Taiwanese chips would lose up to 1.6 trillion dollars of revenue a year in a blockade; over 2 trillion of economic activity at risk (Rhodium Group, “Taiwan economic disruptions”).

Fertilisers. Almost a quarter of world urea exports used to pass through Hormuz (World Bank); for S&P Global the closure of Hormuz removes 29% of world urea trade (Fertilizer Daily, on S&P Global, 20 October 2026). Prices: the World Bank index in April 2026 at its highest since October 2022; urea briefly exceeded $850/t in April (+80% since February, according to press reports on the figure) (World Bank). Middle East fertiliser exports: down 70% because of the war (The National, October 2026).

Wheat. USDA estimates world ending stocks for 2026/27 at 276.0 million tonnes (−2.0%), but the EU falls to 11.7 (−38%), with stocks equal to 10% of consumption; Russia rises to 19.1 (USDA PSD). Ukraine: weekly exports of wheat and oilseeds −90% since early July 2026 (Kpler); the Ukrainian ministry has cut its 2026-27 farm export forecast by 54% (Euromaidan Press). Kerch, latest update: transit permits are issued until 15 November, with a Russian navy escort. (Baird Maritime).

Italy’s dependencies. Italy imports 95.2% of the gas and 89.8% of the oil it consumes (Eurostat, 2024). China is the second-largest supplier of goods with 10.2% of imports (UN Comtrade, 2025). Crude comes mainly from Libya (24.5%), Azerbaijan (15.9%) and Kazakhstan (12.5%); from the Persian Gulf only 5.6–10.2% (UN Comtrade 2025). Data on minerals and components are missing: the files commercio.json and minerali.json reached the observatory on the day of this edition and are not yet integrated into the report.

AssessmentThe bottlenecks of late 2026 are not all at sea. Rare earths (Chinese decision expected on 10 November) and automotive memory chips (capacity absorbed by AI) can halt European production lines more than a high freight rate. For fertilisers the problem is expensive gas in Europe more than logistics. On wheat the EU has its thinnest buffer in years.

7. Digital and physical risks

Subsea cables. Italy has 37 cables and 55 landings; the active cables linking it to Asia through Bab el-Mandeb number 6 (2Africa, Asia Africa Europe-1 (AAE-1), IMEWE, Middle East North Africa (MENA) Cable System/Gulf Bridge International, SeaMeWe-4, SeaMeWe-5). 24 cables run through the Red Sea, 34 through the Baltic (TeleGeography, CC BY-NC-SA, 9 November 2026). The observatory records 14 incidents since 2023, 3 in 2026; the latest: Australia: faults on Indigo West and Indigo Central in the Perth protection zone (8 August 2026, iTnews).

Sabotage and cyber attacks on logistics. The cold chain is the new target: a cyber attack on the refrigeration of hundreds of supermarkets in France (12 October 2026, Al Jazeera); attacks on supermarket refrigeration in Belgium, Luxembourg, Sweden, Ireland and Estonia (2 November 2026, Reuters). Three attacks on Starlink stations in Central and Eastern Europe between September and October (LT, BG, PL): the Lithuanian prime minister links them and attributes them to Russia, as does the Polish government for the facility in Poland (political attributions, not court rulings; ibrido.json). In Italy NoName057(16) claimed DDoS attacks also against Terminal Container Ravenna and Interporto Padova in mid-October, with no confirmation from the victims (QN).

GNSS jamming. GNSS interference along almost all of NATO’s eastern flank from the Baltic to the Black Sea (3 November 2026, Militarnyi, medium reliability). In the Gulf: in Q1 2026 Hormuz, the Persian Gulf and the Gulf of Oman accounted for 98% of GNSS jamming events recorded worldwide, with over 1,100 ships affected; AIS positions shifted onto airports and inland (Windward, “What is GPS jamming and why are we seeing so much of it?”, April 2026). In the observatory’s AIS data 4 ships show impossible positions (over 40 knots or inland), a sign of spoofing or jamming (navi.json, 9 November 2026).

Shadow fleet. 2,022 ships are sanctioned by the US, the UK or the EU (950 linked to Russia, 679 to Iran); 1,163 are assessed as “probable shadow fleet” by the observatory (OFAC, FCDO, OpenSanctions, lists as of 6 November 2026). In the Gulf of Finland 38 of the 96 tankers seen on AIS are sanctioned (Digitraffic and aisstream, 9 November 2026).

AssessmentThe most likely physical risk for Italy is not a cable cut in the Mediterranean but an incident in the Red Sea (anchors of stricken ships, as in 2024) that degrades capacity to Asia and the Gulf, with slow repairs in a war zone. The new risk for European logistics is cyber attacks on control systems (refrigeration, terminals) and GNSS interference in the Baltic and the Gulf, which makes the AIS data these numbers also rely on less reliable.

Gas. On 8 November storage is 63.5% full in the Netherlands (18.9 points below the 5-year average) and 58.9% in Germany (29.7 points below), against 88.9% in Italy (GIE AGSI+, scorte.json). Imported LNG comes mainly from the United States: the Netherlands 77% from the US and 12% from Russia; Germany 89% from the US; Poland 75% from the US and 21% from Qatar (IEEFA European LNG Tracker, May 2026, Q1 2026). Of these, Poland is the most exposed to a halt in Qatari LNG. Denmark is a net gas exporter (import dependency −17.5% in 2024, Eurostat) and a transit country for Baltic Pipe between Norway and Poland (esposizione-nord.json). 8.1–9.4% of the crude imported by the Netherlands comes from the Persian Gulf (UN Comtrade 2025).

AssessmentFor a North Sea port the Hormuz crisis arrives through price more than volumes. Rates to Northern Europe are lower than to the Mediterranean and the return to Suez adds capacity on the Asia–Europe route; Dutch and German LNG comes mainly from the United States, not the Gulf. The weak point is gas: with Dutch and German storage well below average, TTF (which is formed in the Netherlands itself) stays sensitive to every piece of news on Qatar and Hormuz and to a cold winter. For those operating in the Baltic and the Danish Straits the risk is hybrid: cables, the shadow fleet and GNSS interference, which also affects the vessels that install and service offshore wind farms. The data do not say why transits through the Danish Straits are falling: lower Russian exports, sanctions or ships with AIS off may all play a part.

9. Indicators to watch in the coming weeks

Two readings side by side. Chokepoint flows: the same references and thresholds as the risk dashboard (report/metodo-rischio.md): green if the fall against the reference is within 10%, amber between −10% and −30%, red beyond −30%; below −50% the method treats the disruption as ongoing. Prices, freight, diesel and storage: the report uses level thresholds, i.e. what it costs today to import or ship; the risk dashboard instead measures changes (against the 90-day average and against a year earlier). 10. Method and limits

How it is made. Text, tables and charts are generated by report/costruisci_supply_chain.py, which reads the day’s files in docs/data/ and can be re-run every month. Data figures are not typed by hand. Facts the files do not contain (diesel, rare earths, memory, GNSS in the Gulf, freight a year ago, the Baltic Dry, annual port throughput) sit in a table in the script with source, link, date and reliability, and the script warns when they age. Parts marked Assessment are the observatory’s reasoning on the sources cited, not facts.

  • PortWatch counts only ships with AIS on, about a week late: at Hormuz and in the Black Sea “dark” transits are missed (Lloyd’s List Intelligence counts many more transits at Hormuz).
  • Port calls count ships, not tonnes or TEU; over 7 days the numbers are small and swing.
  • Freight rates have no free historical series: the year-on-year WCI comparison uses a value taken from a secondary source. The Baltic Dry is no longer collected automatically (Trading Economics terms of use): it is quoted by hand, a single value, with no year-on-year change.
  • Prices (Brent, TTF, Henry Hub, equities): futures from Yahoo Finance, not official prices; the observatory publishes only about 3 months of series, so year-on-year and year-to-date changes come from the “var” field computed at collection and cannot be recalculated from the file. The latest value on the reading day is provisional.
  • Price level thresholds are assessments; transits use the risk-dashboard method thresholds. Hormuz and Qatari LNG share the same transits, and Brent and TTF appear in several dashboard units: the levels do not add up.
  • The railways are hand-curated profiles: volumes are annual or quarterly and some sources are partisan.
  • Missing: the electricity price (PUN), China–Europe volumes bound for Italy, Italian dependencies by mineral and component (commercio.json and minerali.json exist but are not yet read by the report), current urea and wheat prices from a primary source.
  • Some external sources were not opened (Trading Economics, Motor1, Windward, Riviera, Railmarket): those facts are read from search results and flagged with medium or low reliability. The sources opened during review are listed in the final box.
  • The Northern Europe section uses ports downloaded from PortWatch by the report script and the files paesi.json, scorte.json, navi.json and cavi-incidenti.json: country indicators are missing for Denmark (it is not in paesi.json).

Glossary. AIS: transponder through which ships broadcast position and identity; those that switch it off disappear from the counts. FEU / TEU: 40 / 20-foot container. WCI, FBX, SCFI, CCFI: container freight indices from Drewry, Freightos and the Shanghai Shipping Exchange. Baltic Dry: freight index for dry-bulk ships (ores, coal, grain). LNG: liquefied natural gas. TTF: the benchmark gas price in Europe, traded on the Dutch hub. Brent: the benchmark crude price. Shadow fleet: old tankers with opaque ownership, used to evade sanctions. GNSS jamming / spoofing: interference with or falsification of satellite positioning signals. Port call: a ship’s arrival in port, estimated by PortWatch from AIS.
